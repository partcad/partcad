# //pub/examples/partcad/feature_simulate_joints

PartCAD example project which demonstrates simulating a mechanism, checked against its closed form.

## Usage
A **mechanism** is an assembly whose parts move against each other on
their joints, and what it does can be worked out on paper. This package
states one -- a pendulum -- and holds the simulation to the answer:

```shell
pc sim -a pendulum   # released horizontal, it passes the bottom at the speed its fall gives it
pc sim -a nudged     # swinging 5 degrees, it keeps the small-angle period
pc test -f sim       # both, as the check pc test runs
```

`pendulum` is a 300 x 20 x 10 mm bar hung by one end from a frame. The frame implements `axle`, whose `motion: revolute` says that a
bar joined to it keeps its turn about the axle once it is joined; so the
connection in `pendulum.assy` is a **joint**, named `swing`, and the bar
is a rigid body of its own hanging off the frame's. Everything else in an
assembly is attached: there are two bodies here because there is one
joint. `pc info -a pendulum` lists it under `Joints`.

`toParams: {turnZ: ...}` is where the joint **starts**, and the joint's
zero is the parameter at zero -- here, hanging straight down. `pendulum`
starts at 90 degrees, horizontal, and `nudged` is the same file started at
5: `pc render` draws each where it starts, and the simulation starts it
there too, so a reading of the joint is its value as PartCAD states it.

### The world bolts it down

An assembly placed in a scene is one tree of bodies whose root is free,
so in the scene PartCAD ships the whole pendulum would fall. `bolted` is a
scene that places its subject `fixed: true`: the root body -- the frame --
is welded to the world, and the joint inside it still moves.

### The closed form

For a bar of length L and thickness h turning about the end of its
centre line, everything about the swing follows from L, h and g -- the
mass cancels, so the material does not matter:

| | Formula | Here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| moment of inertia about the axle, over the mass | I/m = L^2/3 + h^2/12 | 0.0300083 m^2 |
| the centre of mass, below the axle | d = L/2 | 0.15 m |
| speed through the bottom, released horizontal | sqrt(2 g d / (I/m)) | 9.9032 rad/s = 567.41 deg/s |
| time to the bottom, released horizontal | K(sin 45 deg) sqrt((I/m) / (g d)) | 0.26477 s |
| the small-angle period | 2 pi sqrt((I/m) / (g d)) | 0.89726 s |

K is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind; K(sin 45 deg) is
1.854075, which is why a pendulum released horizontal takes 18 % longer
to get to the bottom than the small-angle formula says. Each validation
below writes its formula out, and samples the run every millisecond so
that the instants it compares are not a matter of where a reading fell.

MuJoCo's own `before`, `after` and `samples` carry `joints.swing`, in
degrees and deg/s:

```shell
pc sim --json -a pendulum
```
